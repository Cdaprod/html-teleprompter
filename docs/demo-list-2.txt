Pretext is a funny thing.

Something as simple as eye contact in a gas station
gets reclassified as suspicion.
Result: escalation.

A voluntary encounter doesn’t end when a crime is suspected.
It ends the moment refusal becomes cause.

That moment appears in every encounter you’re about to see.
Different officers.
Different agencies.
Same structure.

Conversation becomes interdiction.
Not evidence.
Not crime.

Cause: expected compliance.

Not attitude.
Not escalation.

Pretext.

And pretext is easy to deny in the moment.

That’s why this isn’t about what was said.
It’s about what was recorded.
And what wasn’t.

So I pulled my public record.

Not to argue.
Not to accuse.

Purpose: documentation.

To see how "nothing happened"
gets logged when something clearly did.

(Focus shift.)

Before the encounters you’re about to watch,
I was incarcerated twice in the early 2010s.

Those incidents predate the footage I have today.

What remains are records of outcomes.
Convictions.
Charges.

What’s missing:
The stops.
The detentions.
The escalation.

Cause remains unclear.

Based on lived experience,
and based on what the records show -- and omit --
those earlier incarcerations follow the same pattern you’re about to see.

Extended detention.
Expanded scope.
Fishing.

Back then, I didn’t dissuade it.
Today, I do.

Same playbook.
Different outcome.

ACT II -- THE RECORD

I have questions to underscore.

How do I obtain local police policy?
What search terms were excluded?
What sub-departments were not queried?
Does policy say AGGRESSIVE like the side of the cars?
Does it reflect constitutional enforcement?
Why is the response padded with fluff?
Why are encounters missing detail?
Why are traffic stops absent?

I shouldn’t need dates for "any and all."

I know the information exists.
Detectives followed leads to find me.
Result: no charges filed.

Yet records remain incomplete.

So I document from memory.
Thirteen years or not.

Game on.
I start here.

I’m here because I couldn’t ignore this.
Cause: my name.

I used to do things because I could.
I learned the cost.

"The best rulers are those who have no desire to rule."
-- Plato

Nothing here is a test.
It’s an observation.

I document what I experience.
For the record.

What makes someone reach for authority without need?

Suspicion.

And suspicion is not cause.

Once authority is applied,
it doesn’t disappear.
It follows.

Before I understood the law,
before any redemption arc,
I was subjected to illegal seizures.

Not mistakes.
Power plays.

By people who didn’t see a person.
They saw a target.

But this isn’t about that.

When I was incarcerated,
I knew the ends didn’t justify the means.

Purpose was simple:
Remove this guy from the street.

Shake down.
Lock up.

People say I should write a book.

This isn’t that.

This is a documentary.

Cause: public record.
Result: contradiction.

Apparently, no one’s written it yet.

ACT III -- PATTERN

What becomes unconstitutional
when authority is applied without purpose?

My name is David.

I’ve rebuilt my life for over ten years.

Not because the system fixed me.
Because I fixed myself.

I owe no redemption to the justice system.
I owe everything to my triplets.

That context matters.
This isn’t theory.

This is lived experience.

This country was built on limited power.
Questioned power.
Justified power.

That principle didn’t expire.
It doesn’t shrink because the moment feels small.

I didn’t arrive here as an idea.
I arrived through encounters.
Consequences.
Hindsight.

People ask:
If you did nothing wrong, why does it matter?

Because rights don’t exist for the guilty.
They exist to protect the innocent from unchecked power.

Every name check adds weight.
Invisible.
Persistent.

After years of warrants,
now clean,
I want my work judged on merit.

But aggressive policing doesn’t let the past stay buried.

The Fourth Amendment isn’t a sliding scale.
It applies.
Or it doesn’t.

This isn’t about attitude.
Not escalation.

It’s about process.

And accountability lives in process.

You can feel it in the approach.
They aren’t looking for crime.
They’re looking for reason.

At what point does a bathroom break
become an investigation?

I’m not accused.
Not suspected.

Yet I’m not free to leave.

That’s a seizure.

The moment compliance is required,
voluntary ends.

Without reasonable suspicion,
detention is unlawful.

That’s not opinion.
That’s settled law.

People say:
It’s just your name.
It shouldn’t matter.

That belief is the fuel.

Detain first.
Run later.
See what turns up.

That’s not policing.
That’s fishing.

The Constitution exists to stop that exact behavior.

There’s a fictional middle ground.
They call it consensual.

In reality,
detention is power.

And power leaves marks.

If I did nothing wrong,
I want the smallest footprint possible.

ACT IV -- JEOPARDY REVEAL

So I filed a records request.

Not attack.
Not distortion.

Purpose: truth.

If the system works,
the record should reflect reality.

Clearly.
Completely.
Accurately.

Cost: two hundred dollars.
Volume: hundreds of pages.

Result:
Critical omissions.

What scares me isn’t accountability.
It’s incomplete truth.

I’ve lived when survival becomes routine.
When routine becomes identity.

There was a time my existence was criminal.
Not by desire.
By circumstance.

I carried that version for years.
I earned the consequences.

But that’s not who I am now.

Today:
Breakfast.
Homework.
Building things.

Three boys.
Triplets.

They don’t know that version of me.

But databases do.
Assumptions do.
Half-written records do.

When authority reaches backward
without context,
without justification,
it doesn’t punish who I was.

It reaches for who I am now.

This isn’t erasure.
It’s accuracy.

Redemption only matters
if the system can recognize it.

Not for me.

For my kids.
